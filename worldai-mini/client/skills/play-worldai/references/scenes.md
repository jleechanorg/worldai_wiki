# Scene craft, requested cutscenes and pacing

Use when the player asks for a cutscene, montage, viewpoint change, dramatic beat or different pacing. Normal play stays centered on the player's decisions. A cutscene is a presentation choice, not a license to control the protagonist or reveal everything the GM knows.

## Keep ordinary scenes moving

Start from the current moment. Refer briefly to the last result when useful, then add a fresh observable beat. Do not repeat the encounter introduction, recite the whole quest or restart a settled exchange each turn. Use specific surroundings, physical actions and distinct voices rather than interchangeable atmosphere.

Vary pressure with the fiction. Quiet discovery, practical preparation, celebration and uneventful travel can matter. Do not create a mandatory three-act rhythm, cliffhanger quota, escalating catastrophe or artificial obstacle after every success. Match the player's requested tone. The default scene length is about 250 words / 2–3 short paragraphs; a dialogue-heavy turn may run to about 500 words, and only a heavily dialogue-driven turn to about 1000. Longer narration is appropriate when the player explicitly asks for it, not a reason to postpone the next choice indefinitely.

Ground exits and options in actual time, place and people. The player can reject every suggestion. Avoid options that secretly execute the same event or require an unchosen emotion, confession or betrayal. Always include the core skill's visible **Planning choices** block, even after a concise open question or a requested cutscene. Use the current playable viewpoint for options and preserve any unresolved action.

## Requested cutscene

First determine the requested subject and viewpoint from context. Ask only if the difference matters, such as whether an off-screen scene should reveal secrets. An explicit request for the villain's private council can authorize that fictional spoiler for this player; a request for "a cinematic moment" does not authorize unrelated hidden revelations. This never authorizes disclosure of another person's private information.

For a requested off-screen scene, use that scene's established location and cast. The protagonist does not need to be present; do not fabricate their arrival or omit relevant established participants just to make the scene resemble ordinary play.

Keep the scope bounded:

- Use established facts and characters, adding ordinary fictional detail only where it does not contradict continuity.
- Do not author the protagonist's unchosen dialogue, thoughts, motives, major actions, relationships or resource expenditure. Describe what they perceive and what other characters do; pause when their response matters.
- A request to dramatize the player's already-declared action allows richer depiction of that action, not a different decision or guaranteed success. A contested outcome still follows the agreed resolution method.
- If a flashback would establish a new consequential past fact or commitment, ask the player to choose it rather than asserting it as recovered history. A requested memory scene does not prove that an unknown event occurred.
- End the cutscene at a concrete return point. Reorient to who is present, where, when and what remains pending. Keep established resources and tactical state intact.

## Viewpoint and information

Default to what the player character perceives. For an explicitly requested off-screen or omniscient interlude, label the viewpoint shift clearly and distinguish player knowledge from character knowledge. Do not let the protagonist or an absent companion act on the revealed information without an in-world source.

A cinematic aside need not advance time in the active scene; state the relation when it matters. If the player asks for a concurrent scene, do not use it to resolve their pending combat turn, spend their reaction or progress a deadline by surprise. If an off-screen event establishes a new world fact, retain its time and who witnessed it, while preserving which characters remain unaware.

Checkpoints use the server's player-only export. Never export GM notes or bypass an export refusal with a handwritten spoiler-inclusive dump. A fictional interlude does not authorize file sharing or publication. Keep player-known facts in player state; keep unrevealed notes separate.

## Montage and time skips

Summarize declared routine activity within an agreed scope. Do not decide a contested bargain, a dangerous detour, an attack, a new vow or a major relationship change for the player. Stop before a consequential new choice. If the requested skip crosses a known deadline or risks scarce resources, surface that implication before committing the skip when it is not already clear from their instruction.

On return, summarize only actual changes: elapsed time, location, relevant resources and developments the character can know. Do not reward a montage for its prose length or manufacture off-screen achievements. Use the chosen rules for recovery, training and other mechanical gains.

## Example boundaries

- "Show my character crossing the empty bridge in a cinematic way" permits sensory description of that declared crossing, not an invented ambush or a speech pledging allegiance.
- "Cut to the antagonist planning the raid" permits that fictional off-screen viewpoint; it does not inform the protagonist or automatically make the raid succeed.
- "Fast-forward our safe walk to the inn" permits routine travel; it does not permit spending the last coins on a room or accepting a stranger's deal.
- "What would a dramatic escape look like?" is planning, not an executed escape or a spent spell.
